01 Start here

Everyone had the same flat. That is what makes the memories so specific. ДОМА is an interactive piece about how identical Soviet panel homes became thousands of different ones, and about what people still remember of them when everything else has faded.

If you only read one thing

  • What it is: a personal HCI project, an interactive 3D piece about emotion and memory in the Khrushchyovka, the mass-produced flat that gave millions of families a home of their own.
  • Whose memory: not mine. I never lived in one of these buildings. I have been inside them, invited into other people’s homes, and this is built from what I was shown there and what their owners remember.
  • What to do: close these notes and scroll; nine chapters take about five minutes. Then turn the Memory dial (bottom left) and watch the place fade until only the shape of the building is left.

It is an artistic composite of late-Soviet mass housing, not a measured reconstruction of any one building or any one family’s flat. The light is borrowed from the Zone: the damp, green-grey overcast of late-seventies Soviet cinema.

02 Project outline

The brief: pick an iconic design and the mind behind it; outline, test, build and reflect. I approached it as an HCI project about how architecture shapes behaviour, and how interaction can recreate the feeling of remembering a place.

The design

The standard Soviet prefab flat: the large-panel housing system that, after the 1955 decree “On the Elimination of Excesses in Design and Construction”, set out to give every family a flat of its own. Its template is usually credited to engineer Vitaly Lagutenko’s K-7 series (Workshop No. 7, Mosproekt). Series such as 1-464 (Giprostroiindustriya, 1958–59) carried the same kit of factory-made panels across the country. The tribute is to the work, not the name: a method that turned housing into an assembly of parts.

Problem statement

From outside, mass housing is remembered as grey repetition. From inside, the people who live there remember something else: the carpet on the wall, the kitchen table, the balcony full of jars. Can interaction design recreate how a place is remembered (faded, partial, emotional) rather than how it looked, and make a visitor feel how the architecture shaped the lives inside it?

The insight

The architecture was identical, so the differences were all human: how a family decorated, where they put the things they were given, what made it home. And the architecture itself created behaviour. With a six-square-metre kitchen, the kitchen became the place to talk and gossip late into the night. With no storage indoors, the balcony became the cellar and the attic. These habits did not happen everywhere; they happened there, because of the building.

Design principles

  1. Memory fades, form survives. Turn the Memory dial and colour drains, detail melts into brush strokes and the edges of the frame go first. But the building never disappears: a huge rectangle, rows of identical windows. The simplest, most repeated architecture is also the most unforgettable.
  2. Sameness makes the differences visible. The as designed ↔ as lived slider strips a flat back to what the state delivered, then fills it with what a family brought in. The architecture is the constant; home is the variable.
  3. Architecture creates behaviour. Stories are placed in the rooms that produced them: gossip in the kitchen, storage on the balcony, one room serving as dining room, guest room and bedroom at once.
  4. A guest’s view. The visitor enters as I did, as someone invited in: through the shared stairwell, into a flat that belongs to someone else, hearing the radio before seeing the kitchen.
  5. Less text, more place. Words are short fragments hung in the space, like thoughts rather than captions, and the interface fades when you stop reaching for it.

Key questions

  1. Can interaction recreate remembering a place, rather than documenting it?
  2. Can a standard be humane? What did the system decide, and what did residents decide?
  3. How do you show a method rather than a monument?
  4. Can spatial storytelling (scrolling, walking, sound) convey scale, repetition and intimacy in one piece?
  5. How much atmosphere is needed before documentation turns into memory?

Assumptions

  • Two audiences: people who lived in these flats and will recognise them, and people who never did but can still feel the “everyone had this” familiarity.
  • A composite, carefully sourced flat communicates more than a measured replica of one address.
  • Restraint (short texts placed in space) keeps attention on the building.
  • Figures come from published sources and are marked where schematic.

Chosen approach

An interactive 3D spatial journal in the browser. A scroll-driven walk moves from the microdistrict, up the stairwell and through flat № 6, then across the road to watch a new block assembled panel by panel. Text floats in the scene like placards in a museum; the palette follows the overcast light of Tarkovsky’s Stalker; gaussian splats carry atmosphere and a painterly look. Comparisons make the argument: the flat as designed and as lived; flat № 6 beside a typical 1-464 plan and a kommunalka room. Sound (a radio in the kitchen, footsteps in the stairwell) places the visitor inside.

03 Process & proofs of concept

What was tried, and what each test decided.

Point cloud
A world made only of splats read as noise. Learned: forms must stay solid.
Solid rebuild
Realism and a coherent plan made the flat believable; the dead-end corridor and stair were cut. Kept.
World scan
Splats on every surface looked like a strange texture, not memory. Rejected.
Painterly strokes
Repainting each frame in oriented gaussian strokes kept colour whole and matched the reference. Kept, with sliders.
Type in the scene
Large wall-mounted plates felt pasted on; short floating fragments along the route read as a museum walk. Kept.

04 MVP & reflection

Minimum viable product

  • Nine chapters, from the block to the construction site and a housewarming in the new flat, reachable by scroll or the Chapters menu.
  • The Memory dial: one control from “now” to “only the shape”, fading colour, detail, focus and sound together.
  • Walk, free camera, four seasons that change the light indoors and out, indoor haze and paint controls.
  • As designed ↔ as lived; the plan placard; the assembly of a 1-464-type section with flat № 6 inside it.
  • Positional sound; these field notes with sources.

Reflection, so far

The most important decisions were subtractive: removing texture from the splats, shortening every text, letting the interface recede. Realism made the space believable; feeling came from light, pacing and restraint. Next, an evaluation that fits the question: short sessions with people who grew up in these flats and people who did not. Ask what each recognises, what they remember, and when the Memory dial stops feeling like a filter and starts feeling like remembering. Then verify every figure against a second source and test on a mid-range laptop.

05 Working log / Журнал

Eight iterations, oldest first. Open any entry to read it.

№ 001 The scan

The first version treated everything as a point cloud. The block was there, but it dissolved: every wall and cushion read as dots, and the rooms floated as three disconnected boxes joined by a corridor that led nowhere.

№ 002 Solid first, air second

Surfaces became real materials again; the gaussian splats moved off the walls and into the air — leaves, far-off towers dissolving in haze, dust hanging indoors. The three boxes became one plausible two-room flat off a single landing, and the stair was rebuilt to climb, flight after flight, to the roof.

№ 003 The Zone

Golden hour felt like a postcard, so the light went cold and wet: overcast, cyan-green, lifted blacks, puddles on the asphalt. Four seasons change the place itself — snow on every sill, spring blossom, June’s poplar fluff, autumn litter. The indoor haze became a dial, and the flat became something you can walk through yourself.

№ 004 Paint, not texture

A first attempt scattered textured splats over every surface; it read as a strange pattern rather than a memory. Studying painterly scan walkthroughs showed the real idea: the whole frame is repainted in soft, overlapping gaussian brush strokes that follow the image itself, so colour stays whole and forms stay readable while everything else melts. An autofocus keeps what you look at crisp. Splat level, stroke visibility, brush size and focus are yours to set.

№ 005 Words in the world

The captions left the page. Each chapter is set as type in the place it describes, and it assembles as you arrive and comes apart as you leave. Its colours are sampled from the frame, so the type wears the season and the room. The rest of the interface steps back when you stop reaching for it.

№ 006 A museum walk, and the method

Long captions became short fragments hung along the way, like placards in a gallery. The flat can be seen as designed — bare walls, standard fittings — or as lived; a plan on the landing sets it beside a typical 1-464 flat and a kommunalka room. After the balcony the walk crosses the road to a construction site, because the brilliance of this design was never one building but the method. Sound joined in: a radio in the kitchen, footsteps in the stairwell, weather outside.

№ 007 The same room, beginning again

The site follows the real sequence: foundation, then each floor as slabs, wall panels with windows fitted, interior walls, a bathroom cabin lowered whole, the stair, the facade. The walk now ends inside the new block: you climb in through the living-room window and watch the flat become the room you stood in — whitewash, parquet, paper, then the family’s things one by one, until the lamp comes on and the radio starts again. The kitchen is lit like a memory, soft and milky, as if someone were dreaming it. The seasons stopped being four kinds of overcast: summer has a high sun and warm light pouring in; spring is fresh and bright; autumn keeps the Stalker grey; winter is cold daylight with the lamps on early. The balcony got its steel railing back.

№ 008 Only the shape

The project found its frame: not a history lesson but a piece about memory, and not my memory but the memories of the people who live there, whose homes I was invited into. The paint controls became one Memory dial. As it turns, colour drains to sepia, brush strokes grow, nothing stays in focus, the edges of the frame are forgotten first and the sound goes muffled, as if heard through a wall. At the far end only the shape is left: a pale rectangle with rows of windows. That is the point. Everything else fades, but you still remember the building.

06 Why it looked like this / Почему так

Mass housing
A 1955 decree against “excesses” ended ornament; from 1957 the promise was a separate flat for every family. Factory-made panels made that possible at speed, and made every block alike.
The stairwell
Washable oil paint to shoulder height, whitewash above; a lift only where the norms demanded it, above five storeys.
The kitchen
Planned tiny on the assumption that cooking would move to canteens; it became the heart of the home and of late-night conversation instead.
The carpet and the stenka
Warmth and sound-proofing for thin panel walls, but also status and savings in an economy of shortages; the wall unit showed off crystal and subscription book sets.
The bedroom set and the pillows
Furniture came as a matching set after long waiting lists; the lace-covered pyramid of pillows travelled from village dowries into city flats.
The balcony
Cellar, attic, pantry and drying room in one, and, glazed by hand, the only way to grow a standard flat.

  • Technique: one continuous, fully modelled environment (a twelve-storey panel tower, its stair core and one furnished flat) with physically based materials and weather computed in the materials. Gaussian splats carry foliage, haze, snow, rain and dust; a painterly layer re-draws each frame as gaussian brush strokes aligned with the image (an anisotropic Kuwahara filter).
