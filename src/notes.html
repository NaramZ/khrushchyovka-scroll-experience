Personal work · an interactive memory · design + direction mine, built with Claude

A walk into a late-Soviet panel flat, and out again into the building next door, where the same flat is being made for somebody else. It isn’t about what these buildings looked like. It’s about what people remember of them.

How to read this: close the entry and scroll first; it takes about five minutes. Then come back. Or read on, and turn the Memory knob (bottom left) when you close it.

01 Start with the wrong question

I started where everyone starts with Soviet housing: the grey block, the repetition, the thousand identical windows. I built the tower, the stairwell, the courtyard, and it looked right. It didn’t feel like anything.

What did these buildings look like?→What do the people who live there remember?

These aren’t my memories. I never lived in one of these buildings. I have been inside them, though: up the shared stairwell and into other people’s homes, as a guest. What stayed with me wasn’t the architecture. It was how completely each family had made the same flat their own.

the test: if someone who grew up in one of these flats says “we had that exact carpet”, it worked.

02 The same flat, everywhere

After a 1955 decree against “excesses” in architecture, the Soviet state promised every family a flat of its own. The answer was a method, not a building: concrete panels cast in a factory, trucked to site, craned into place floor by floor. Engineer Vitaly Lagutenko’s K-7 series is usually credited as the template; series like 1-464 carried the same kit across the country. Millions of people got the same rooms.

That sameness is the interesting part. When the architecture is identical, every difference is human: where the sofa went, which carpet hung on the wall, what lived in the cabinet behind glass. And the building shaped the life inside it.

  • A six-square-metre kitchenbecame the place to talk, gossip and argue late into the night: the warmest, most private room in the flat.
  • No storage indoorsturned the balcony into the cellar, the attic and the pantry, and later, glazed by hand, into an extra room.
  • One main roomhad to be dining room, guest room and, with the sofa unfolded, a bedroom.

Those habits didn’t happen everywhere. They happened there, because of the building.

03 Designing for memory

Every interaction had to mean something about remembering, or it didn’t go in.

04 How I worked with Claude

This was built with Claude as a design-engineering pair. The direction was mine: the idea, the references (photographs of real flats, a still from Stalker, the period radio recordings), the art direction, and every round of critique. Claude turned that into code I could react to, fast.

Most of the work was reacting. The balcony didn’t look like a balcony. The kitchen was too bright, then too dark. The text stuck to the walls like signage. Spring looked like a different winter. Each note sent it back, and the piece got closer to what I remembered seeing.

the speed was the tool’s. what to keep was mine.

05 What got scrapped, and what it taught me

The first version was all point cloud. Every wall and cushion dissolved into dots, and the rooms floated as three boxes joined by a corridor that went nowhere. It looked like data, not a home. Forms had to stay solid.

Next I scattered textured splats over every surface to get a “scanned” look. It read as a strange pattern, not a memory. What worked was the opposite: keep the world solid and repaint the whole frame in soft brush strokes, so colour stays whole and only detail melts.

The text started as big plates stuck to the walls. They felt pasted on, so they became short fragments hanging in the air, several per room, like placards in a museum.

Golden hour went too. It felt like a postcard. The light went cold and wet instead.

Log Every iteration, in order
  1. The scan. Everything as a point cloud; rooms as disconnected boxes.
  2. Solid first, air second. Real materials; splats moved into the air as leaves, haze and dust. One plausible flat off a single landing; a stair that climbs.
  3. The Zone. Overcast light, four seasons, a first-person walk.
  4. Paint, not texture. The painterly layer, with an autofocus that keeps what you look at crisp.
  5. Words in the world. Type placed in the rooms; colours sampled from the frame.
  6. A museum walk, and the method. Short fragments; as designed ↔ as lived; the plan on the landing; the construction site; sound.
  7. The same room, beginning again. The real assembly sequence; the ending in the new flat; seasons that change the light indoors; a balcony that’s actually a balcony.
  8. Only the shape. The project found its frame, memory, and the paint controls became one Memory knob.

07 What I’d do differently

I’d start with the question from section 01 instead of arriving at it. I spent the early rounds perfecting how the building looked, when what mattered was how it’s remembered. Designing for the feeling first would have saved the point cloud, the scan and most of the golden hour.

And I’d test it with the people it’s about. The next step is short sessions with two groups: people who grew up in these flats, and people who never did. What does each recognise? When does the Memory knob stop feeling like a filter and start feeling like remembering? That answer decides whether this works.

08 Notes from the rooms

The stairwell
Washable oil paint to shoulder height, whitewash above; a lift only where the norms demanded one, above five storeys.
The kitchen
Planned tiny on the assumption that cooking would move to canteens. It became the heart of the home instead.
The carpet and the stenka
Warmth and sound-proofing for thin panel walls, but also savings and status in an economy of shortages. The wall unit showed off crystal and subscription book sets.
The bedroom
Furniture came as a matching set after long waiting lists; the lace-covered pyramid of pillows travelled from village dowries into city flats.
The balcony
Cellar, attic, pantry and drying room in one, and, glazed by hand, the only way to grow a standard flat.
